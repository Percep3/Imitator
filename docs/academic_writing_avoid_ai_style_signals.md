# Academic Writing Style Guide for AI-Assisted Conference Papers

## Purpose

Use these instructions when drafting, rewriting, or polishing academic papers in English.

The goal is **not** to game AI detectors. The goal is to avoid recurrent stylistic artifacts associated with generic LLM prose while preserving the qualities expected from a strong conference paper: precision, technical clarity, evidential discipline, compact argumentation, and professional academic tone.

The guidance is adapted from Wikipedia's *Signs of AI writing* field guide, with the rules narrowed to patterns that matter most in research papers.

> **Important:** No individual phrase proves that a text was generated by AI. Human writers use many of these constructions naturally. The problem is their **density, regularity, repetition, and use without evidential or rhetorical necessity**.

---

# 1. Core writing objective

Write like a researcher reporting a specific study, not like a generic system explaining a topic.

Every paragraph should do at least one concrete job:

- state a research problem;
- define an assumption;
- describe a method;
- report evidence;
- compare results;
- explain a mechanism;
- qualify a claim;
- connect the current result to prior work;
- establish a limitation.

Prefer **specific claims supported by data or citations** over generic statements about importance, impact, innovation, or broader trends.

---

# 2. Highest-priority patterns to avoid

These patterns deserve the strongest attention during revision because repeated use produces a highly recognizable LLM-like rhythm.

## 2.1. Repeated negative parallelisms

Avoid repeatedly framing claims as corrections to an imaginary misconception.

Common templates:

- `not only X, but also Y`
- `not just X, but Y`
- `not merely X; it also Y`
- `it is not X, but rather Y`
- `rather than simply X, Y`
- `X is more than Y`
- `the contribution is not limited to X`

These forms are grammatically valid. The problem is habitual use.

### Bad

> Our method not only improves classification accuracy but also enhances robustness under distribution shift.

### Better

> Our method improves classification accuracy and reduces the accuracy drop under distribution shift.

### Better when quantitative evidence exists

> Accuracy increases from 81.4% to 84.9%, while the corruption-induced drop decreases from 13.2 to 8.7 percentage points.

### Rule

**State the intended claim directly unless a real contrast is necessary for the argument.**

Use `not X but Y` only when:

1. the distinction is analytically important;
2. X is a plausible interpretation that must actually be rejected;
3. the contrast changes how the reader should understand the result.

Do not use negative parallelism merely to make a sentence sound emphatic.

---

## 2.2. Repeated sentence templates

Do not let several consecutive sentences share the same rhetorical skeleton.

Watch for sequences such as:

> X enables Y.  
> X facilitates Z.  
> X enhances W.  
> X ensures Q.

or:

> This highlights...  
> This demonstrates...  
> This underscores...  
> This emphasizes...

or repeated paragraph endings such as:

> ..., highlighting the importance of X.  
> ..., demonstrating the effectiveness of Y.  
> ..., underscoring the relevance of Z.

### Rule

During revision, inspect every sequence of 3–5 sentences.

If the syntax or rhetorical function repeats, restructure the passage rather than replacing verbs with synonyms.

Possible fixes:

- combine two closely related claims;
- replace generic interpretation with a measured result;
- change one sentence into a causal explanation;
- remove redundant commentary;
- make the subject explicit;
- split observations from interpretation.

Variation should emerge from the argument, not from artificial synonym substitution.

---

## 2.3. Superficial `-ing` analysis

Be suspicious of sentence-final participial clauses such as:

- `highlighting ...`
- `underscoring ...`
- `demonstrating ...`
- `emphasizing ...`
- `reflecting ...`
- `showcasing ...`
- `ensuring ...`
- `fostering ...`
- `contributing to ...`
- `providing valuable insights into ...`

These clauses often append an interpretation that is neither necessary nor demonstrated.

### Bad

> The model reaches 87.3% accuracy, highlighting its effectiveness in challenging real-world settings.

The number does not by itself establish either "effectiveness" or "real-world" validity.

### Better

> The model reaches 87.3% accuracy on the evaluation set.

### Better with justified interpretation

> The model reaches 87.3% accuracy, 4.1 points above the strongest baseline under the same evaluation protocol.

### Rule

For every sentence-final `-ing` clause, ask:

> Does this clause add a fact, a supported interpretation, or only rhetorical emphasis?

Delete it when it adds only emphasis.

---

## 2.4. Artificial rule-of-three phrasing

LLMs frequently package claims into groups of three:

- `efficient, robust, and scalable`
- `accuracy, reliability, and interpretability`
- `design, implementation, and evaluation`
- `identify, analyze, and address`
- `complex, dynamic, and evolving`

Three-item lists are legitimate when the content actually has three components. Do not manufacture the third element for rhetorical symmetry.

### Bad

> The proposed framework is efficient, robust, and scalable.

### Better

> Inference requires 18 ms per sample on an RTX 4090. Accuracy decreases by 3.2 points under Gaussian corruption.

If scalability was not evaluated, do not claim it.

### Rule

**The number of items must come from the research, not from prose aesthetics.**

If there are two relevant properties, write two. If there are four, write four.

---

## 2.5. High density of stereotypical LLM vocabulary

The following words are not forbidden. Many are normal technical terms. Their repeated, generic use is the warning sign:

- additionally
- crucial
- pivotal
- key
- robust
- enhance
- highlighting
- emphasizing
- showcasing
- underscore
- fostering
- intricate
- interplay
- landscape
- valuable
- comprehensive
- significant
- notable
- compelling
- promising
- transformative
- groundbreaking
- novel

### Particularly weak academic uses

> a robust and comprehensive framework  
> a crucial challenge  
> the rapidly evolving landscape  
> provides valuable insights  
> plays a pivotal role  
> highlights the importance  
> showcases the effectiveness  
> underscores the need for

### Rule

Do not replace these words mechanically with synonyms.

Instead, ask whether the sentence can be made **more measurable or specific**.

### Example

Instead of:

> The method provides a robust solution for noisy environments.

Write:

> Under 20% label noise, the method retains 78.6% accuracy compared with 69.1% for the baseline.

`Robust` remains acceptable when it denotes a defined research concept, e.g., *robust optimization*, *robustness evaluation*, or *adversarial robustness*.

---

## 2.6. Inflated significance

Avoid generic claims that make ordinary findings sound historically or scientifically momentous.

Watch for:

- `marks a significant shift`
- `represents a major advancement`
- `plays a pivotal role`
- `is crucial for`
- `sets the stage for`
- `has far-reaching implications`
- `reflects a broader trend`
- `addresses a critical gap`
- `paves the way for`
- `opens new avenues`
- `has the potential to revolutionize`

### Bad

> These findings mark a significant advancement in trustworthy AI.

### Better

> These findings show that calibration error decreases under the three tested corruption settings.

### Rule

Claims of importance must be grounded in one of the following:

- a measurable improvement;
- a previously unresolved capability;
- a clearly stated theoretical consequence;
- a documented practical requirement;
- a comparison with prior work.

Do not tell the reader a result is important when you can show why it matters.

---

## 2.7. Formulaic "challenges and future prospects" prose

Avoid generic closing structures such as:

> Despite these promising results, several challenges remain...

followed by:

> Future work could explore...

and then:

> Nevertheless, the proposed approach provides a strong foundation...

A limitations section is appropriate. A generic optimism sandwich is not.

### Better structure

State limitations as concrete constraints:

> The evaluation uses a single dataset and does not measure cross-domain transfer. In addition, the memory module adds 18% inference latency. These results therefore do not establish performance under deployment-scale domain shift.

Future work should follow only when there is a technically motivated next step:

> Cross-domain evaluation is needed to determine whether the observed gain persists when the corruption distribution changes.

Do not end with mandatory optimism.

---

## 2.8. Vague attribution and invented consensus

Avoid:

- `researchers have shown`
- `studies suggest`
- `the literature demonstrates`
- `experts agree`
- `it is widely recognized`
- `recent research has highlighted`
- `several works have explored`

unless the citations actually support the breadth of the statement.

### Better

> Wang et al. [12] report a 2.3-point improvement on CIFAR-100.

> Three recent studies [8, 12, 17] evaluate retrieval-augmented memory under continual-learning settings.

### Rule

Do not turn one citation into a field-wide consensus.

Match the grammatical scope of the claim to the evidence you actually have.

---

# 3. Prefer direct academic syntax

## 3.1. Do not avoid `is`, `are`, `has`, or `uses`

LLMs often replace simple copular constructions with inflated alternatives:

- `serves as`
- `stands as`
- `functions as`
- `represents`
- `features`
- `boasts`
- `offers`
- `maintains`
- `constitutes`

Use the simplest accurate verb.

### Inflated

> The memory bank serves as a mechanism for storing prototype representations.

### Better

> The memory bank stores prototype representations.

### Inflated

> The architecture features three encoder blocks.

### Better

> The architecture has three encoder blocks.

In technical writing, direct verbs usually improve precision.

---

## 3.2. Prefer verbs that describe the actual operation

Weak:

> The module enhances feature representations.

Better:

> The module reweights channel activations using a learned attention vector.

Weak:

> The loss encourages better alignment.

Better:

> The loss minimizes the cosine distance between paired video and text embeddings.

Whenever possible, replace evaluative verbs with **mechanistic verbs**.

---

# 4. Maintain academic rigor without sounding mechanical

## 4.1. Use technical terms consistently

Do not vary terminology merely to avoid repetition.

If `embedding`, `baseline`, `dataset`, `retrieval`, or `corruption` is the correct term, repeat it.

Do not rotate through near-synonyms such as:

> approach / framework / strategy / mechanism / paradigm

unless they refer to different concepts.

Terminological consistency is a strength in scientific writing.

---

## 4.2. Vary sentence length naturally

Do not force every paragraph into similarly sized sentences.

Short sentences are acceptable when they state an important constraint or result.

Longer sentences are appropriate when dependencies or experimental conditions need to remain together.

### Good principle

Use sentence length to reflect information structure.

Do **not** add subordinate clauses merely to sound sophisticated.

---

## 4.3. Do not force transitions between every sentence

Avoid excessive use of:

- Additionally,
- Furthermore,
- Moreover,
- Importantly,
- Notably,
- Consequently,
- In this context,
- In this regard,
- On the other hand,

Transitions should encode a real logical relationship.

Two adjacent sentences often need no transition.

### Example

> We freeze the ResNet-18 backbone during training. The memory module is optimized with AdamW at a learning rate of \(10^{-4}\).

No connector is required.

---

## 4.4. Avoid empty metadiscourse

Remove phrases such as:

- `It is important to note that`
- `It should be noted that`
- `It is worth mentioning that`
- `Interestingly,`
- `Notably,`
- `As can be clearly seen,`
- `In this section, we discuss`
- `The remainder of this section will`
- `It is evident that`

unless they serve a real structural purpose.

State the information directly.

### Weak

> It is important to note that the baseline uses the same encoder.

### Better

> The baseline uses the same encoder.

---

# 5. Evidence-first writing

## 5.1. Prefer quantities over evaluative adjectives

Weak:

> Our model substantially outperforms the baseline.

Better:

> Our model improves Top-1 accuracy from 68.6% to 76.7%.

Weak:

> The model is considerably more robust.

Better:

> Under corruption, accuracy decreases by 9.1 points for our method and 13.7 points for the baseline.

Terms such as `substantially`, `significantly`, or `considerably` should not substitute for measurements.

Use `statistically significant` only when supported by an appropriate statistical test.

---

## 5.2. Separate observation from explanation

Use a clear epistemic distinction.

### Observation

> Accuracy decreases from 84.2% to 78.1% after removing the memory module.

### Supported explanation

> The ablation indicates that the memory module accounts for part of the observed gain.

### Hypothesis

> One possible explanation is that prototype retrieval stabilizes predictions for low-frequency classes.

Do not present a hypothesis as an empirical fact.

---

## 5.3. Preserve negative and inconclusive results

Do not rewrite every outcome into a success narrative.

Acceptable:

> The proposed loss does not improve Recall@10.

> We observe no consistent gain on Flowers-102.

> The difference is within the standard deviation across seeds.

> The experiment does not establish whether the improvement transfers to unseen domains.

Scientific credibility is improved by accurate boundaries, not universal positivity.

---

# 6. Citation discipline

## 6.1. Never fabricate bibliographic information

Never invent or autocomplete:

- authors;
- paper titles;
- venues;
- publication years;
- DOI identifiers;
- page numbers;
- URLs;
- dataset versions.

When a citation is uncertain, mark it for verification rather than guessing.

---

## 6.2. Check claim-to-citation alignment

A citation is not sufficient merely because it concerns the same topic.

For every important citation, verify:

1. the source exists;
2. the bibliographic metadata is correct;
3. the cited work actually supports the sentence;
4. the strength of the sentence does not exceed the source;
5. a result from one dataset is not generalized to all settings.

---

## 6.3. Do not manufacture literature consensus

Bad:

> Prior work has established that memory-based methods are superior for robust classification [12].

if [12] is one paper with one experiment.

Better:

> Smith et al. [12] report higher corruption accuracy for a memory-based classifier on CIFAR-10-C.

---

# 7. Section-specific instructions for conference papers

## 7.1. Abstract

The abstract should normally contain:

1. the specific problem;
2. the concrete methodological idea;
3. the evaluation setting;
4. the main result(s);
5. the supported conclusion.

Avoid generic opening claims such as:

> Artificial intelligence has revolutionized numerous domains.

> In the rapidly evolving landscape of deep learning...

> Robustness is a crucial challenge in modern AI systems.

Start closer to the research question.

### Prefer

> Image classifiers often lose accuracy under common corruptions not represented during training. We evaluate...

Use actual numbers when space allows.

Do not end with:

> These results demonstrate the potential of our approach to advance the field.

State what the results establish.

---

## 7.2. Introduction

Do not build the introduction as a sequence of generic importance claims.

Avoid:

> X has become increasingly important. However, several challenges remain. Recent advances have shown promising results. Nevertheless, a critical gap persists.

Instead:

- define the concrete failure mode;
- explain why existing approaches do not resolve it;
- identify the precise research gap;
- state what the paper tests or contributes.

### Contribution lists

Contribution bullets are normal in many venues, but each bullet must contain a real contribution.

Avoid artificial symmetry:

> We propose...
> We develop...
> We demonstrate...

if the three bullets are different phrasings of the same contribution.

Do not call every contribution `novel`.

---

## 7.3. Related work

Organize related work by technical relationship, not by vague chronology.

Avoid paper-by-paper summaries with repeated templates:

> Smith et al. proposed...
> Jones et al. introduced...
> Lee et al. developed...

Instead, synthesize where evidence permits:

> Memory-based classifiers differ mainly in how prototypes are constructed and updated. Static prototype methods [4, 7] compute class representatives after training, whereas adaptive methods [9, 11] update memory during optimization.

Do not fabricate differences that the cited papers do not establish.

---

## 7.4. Method

This section should be especially resistant to rhetorical filler.

Prefer:

- definitions;
- equations;
- tensor shapes;
- algorithms;
- architectural choices;
- training objectives;
- implementation details.

Avoid:

> To effectively capture rich and meaningful representations...

Prefer:

> The encoder maps each input \(x\) to a 192-dimensional embedding \(z\).

Avoid saying a component is `designed to effectively` do something when you can state what it mathematically computes.

---

## 7.5. Experiments

Report:

- datasets;
- splits;
- baselines;
- metrics;
- hyperparameters relevant to reproducibility;
- number of seeds;
- uncertainty estimates where appropriate;
- statistical tests when used.

Avoid evaluative narration before presenting evidence.

Bad:

> Our method achieves impressive improvements across all benchmarks.

Better:

> Our method improves Top-1 accuracy on three of four benchmarks; Table 2 reports the absolute differences.

If one benchmark regresses, state it.

---

## 7.6. Results and discussion

Do not narrate every table cell.

Focus on:

- comparisons that answer research questions;
- unexpected outcomes;
- trade-offs;
- ablations;
- failure cases;
- uncertainty.

Avoid repeatedly ending result paragraphs with:

> This highlights the effectiveness of our approach.

Let the comparison carry the argument.

---

## 7.7. Limitations

State actual limitations.

Examples:

- one geographic region;
- one dataset;
- small sample size;
- no external validation;
- sensitivity to hyperparameters;
- computational overhead;
- missing modality;
- annotation bias;
- limited demographic coverage;
- untested deployment conditions.

Do not use the limitations section as a transition into promotional future work.

---

## 7.8. Conclusion

A conference-paper conclusion should be compact.

It should usually:

- restate the research question in concrete terms;
- summarize the main empirical finding;
- state the main limitation or scope boundary when relevant.

Avoid:

> In conclusion, our innovative framework represents a significant step toward...

Prefer:

> The proposed memory module improves corruption accuracy by 1.9 points over the frozen-backbone baseline while preserving clean accuracy. The evaluation is limited to two image datasets, so cross-domain robustness remains untested.

---

# 8. Patterns that are allowed when technically justified

Do not degrade academic prose merely to avoid superficial similarity with LLM output.

The following are acceptable when they are the correct expression:

- `robust` when discussing robustness;
- `significant` for statistical significance;
- `key` when referring to a genuinely central variable or result;
- `not X but Y` when an explicit conceptual distinction must be made;
- three-item lists when there are exactly three relevant items;
- passive voice when the actor is irrelevant;
- em dashes when stylistically appropriate;
- technical repetition when terminology must remain stable.

The target is **non-formulaic precision**, not forced informality.

---

# 9. Revision algorithm for an AI writing agent

After drafting, perform the following passes.

## Pass 1 — Claim verification

For every factual or comparative sentence:

- identify its evidence;
- verify that the evidence supports the full claim;
- reduce the claim if support is narrower;
- mark hypotheses explicitly;
- remove invented causal explanations.

Do not continue stylistic polishing until this pass is complete.

---

## Pass 2 — Repetition scan

Search the draft for repeated:

- sentence openings;
- transition words;
- sentence-final participial clauses;
- `not X but Y` constructions;
- `not only ... but also ...`;
- three-part enumerations;
- `This + verb` sentence openings;
- paragraph-ending summary sentences.

If a pattern appears several times in close proximity, rewrite at the structural level.

Do not merely swap words.

---

## Pass 3 — LLM-vocabulary density

Search for:

`additionally`, `crucial`, `pivotal`, `key`, `robust`, `enhance`, `highlight`, `emphasize`, `showcase`, `underscore`, `foster`, `intricate`, `interplay`, `landscape`, `valuable`, `comprehensive`, `significant`, `promising`, `transformative`.

For each occurrence, classify it as:

- **technical/necessary** → keep;
- **supported evaluative term** → keep or quantify;
- **generic emphasis** → remove;
- **replaceable with a fact or measurement** → rewrite.

Do not ban words globally.

---

## Pass 4 — Directness

Search for:

- `serves as`
- `stands as`
- `functions as`
- `represents`
- `features`
- `offers`
- `constitutes`
- `plays a ... role`

Replace with `is`, `has`, `uses`, or a more precise operation whenever meaning is preserved.

---

## Pass 5 — Evidence density

For each paragraph, identify the most concrete item:

- number;
- citation;
- equation;
- mechanism;
- experimental observation;
- definition.

If a paragraph contains only generic interpretation, rewrite it around specific evidence or delete it.

---

## Pass 6 — Academic tone

Remove:

- promotional language;
- motivational language;
- conversational filler;
- rhetorical questions unless the venue/style calls for them;
- generic praise of the method;
- claims about being groundbreaking;
- unsupported claims of practical impact.

Keep the prose neutral and technically assertive.

---

## Pass 7 — Rhythm

Read the paragraph as a sequence of syntactic forms.

Avoid:

- identical sentence lengths;
- four consecutive sentences with the same subject;
- repeated transition + claim structures;
- every paragraph ending in a synthetic takeaway.

Do not deliberately inject errors, slang, awkward grammar, or random sentence fragments to appear human.

---

# 10. Compact instruction block for direct use in an agent

Use the following as persistent writing instructions:

> Write in professional conference-paper English. Prioritize technical specificity, evidence, and concise argumentation over rhetorical polish. Avoid formulaic LLM patterns, especially repeated `not X but Y` or `not only X but also Y` constructions, sentence-final `highlighting/underscoring/demonstrating` clauses, forced three-item lists, generic importance claims, vague attribution, and repeated transition words. Do not manufacture contrasts, consensus, significance, or optimism. Use simple verbs (`is`, `has`, `uses`) when they are more precise than inflated alternatives (`serves as`, `features`, `represents`). Use technical terms consistently rather than rotating synonyms. Prefer measurements, mechanisms, equations, and explicitly sourced claims over evaluative adjectives. Preserve negative or inconclusive results. Distinguish observations, interpretations, and hypotheses. Never invent citations or bibliographic metadata. Vary syntax only when the logical structure of the content warrants it; do not add stylistic irregularity artificially. Maintain the tone of a careful researcher, not a marketing document or a tutorial chatbot.

---

# 11. Final checklist

Before returning a paper section, verify:

- [ ] No citation or bibliographic field was invented.
- [ ] Quantitative claims contain the relevant values when available.
- [ ] The text does not repeatedly use `not X but Y`.
- [ ] `not only ... but also ...` is rare and logically necessary.
- [ ] Three-item lists reflect actual content rather than rhetorical symmetry.
- [ ] Sentence-final `-ing` clauses are factual or justified.
- [ ] There is no repeated `highlight / underscore / emphasize / showcase` pattern.
- [ ] Generic words such as `crucial`, `pivotal`, `robust`, and `significant` are technically justified or replaced with evidence.
- [ ] The text does not exaggerate the broader significance of ordinary results.
- [ ] Claims about prior work match the cited sources.
- [ ] One or two sources are not presented as scientific consensus.
- [ ] Methods are described operationally rather than promotionally.
- [ ] Results include failures and regressions when they occur.
- [ ] Limitations are concrete.
- [ ] Future work is technically motivated, not a mandatory optimistic ending.
- [ ] Terminology remains consistent.
- [ ] Paragraphs do not all share the same sentence structure or ending.
- [ ] No chatbot-style communication remains in the manuscript.
- [ ] The final prose is grammatical, formal, concise, and readable.

# 12. Source and interpretation note

This guide is adapted primarily from the English Wikipedia WikiProject AI Cleanup page **“Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing”**, consulted on 30 September 2026.

Relevant observations from that page include:

- high density of recurrent “AI vocabulary”;
- avoidance of simple copular constructions;
- negative parallelisms such as `not just X, but also Y`;
- overuse of the rule of three;
- superficial analysis attached through participial clauses;
- inflated statements of significance and broader impact;
- vague attribution and overgeneralization;
- formulaic conclusions about challenges and future prospects;
- repeated formatting and structural habits;
- citation and reference failures.

Wikipedia explicitly describes its page as a **descriptive field guide rather than a prescriptive authorship test**. It also warns that AI-detection tools have non-trivial error rates and that no individual stylistic feature proves AI authorship.

For academic writing, the appropriate response is therefore not to mechanically suppress individual words. The stronger strategy is to improve the underlying text: make claims specific, make evidence traceable, keep terminology stable, remove unsupported interpretation, and let the structure follow the research rather than a generic prose template.
